## 2026-10-27 — Signing in stays a server-side session, which lives by its account's tier, and no administrator chooses another's password

1. **A signed-in browser holds a session cookie and nothing else, and the session is a row that names its account.** The cookie is HttpOnly, `__Host-` once deployed, and carries a random key; the session behind it is stored by `apps/accounts/sessions.py` in `AccountSession`, whose columns name the account and the device ([data-model.md](data-model.md)), so ending every session of an account, on deactivation or a password change, is one indexed `DELETE`, and its owner lists and ends them one by one (`apps/accounts/self_views.py`).
   **Why:** the IETF's best practice for browser applications names a single-origin application that keeps its sessions on the server, in cookies, as a pattern of its own outside OAuth's scope (outside the repository: RFC 10017, *OAuth 2.0 for Browser-Based Applications*, section 7.1). The chat applications that keep a token where the page's script can read it pay for it: Open WebUI writes its token to `localStorage` beside the cookie, and an advisory of 27 September 2026 let any site take a signed-in user's (outside the repository: GHSA-vpq8-f445-hcq7); AnythingLLM and RAGFlow keep theirs there too, AnythingLLM's for 30 days with no way to revoke it. Onyx keeps an opaque token in a server-side store behind an HttpOnly cookie, the model kept here (outside the repository: their source, October 2026 releases). Django's own table stores a session as an encoded blob, so finding an account's sessions means decoding every row, as Zulip does (outside the repository: `zerver/lib/sessions.py`); Django's documented way out is a session model of one's own (outside the repository: Django, *How to use sessions*, "Extending database-backed session engines").
   **The price:** the new table starts empty, so everyone is signed out once when it is migrated.
2. **A session ends at its tier's limits: a student's after 7 days idle or 14 in all, a staff one's, a superuser's or a role holder's, after 1 hour idle or 24 in all.** `SESSION_LIFETIMES` in `config/settings.py`, held by `apps/accounts/middleware.py`, which reads the tier on every request, so a role granted mid-session holds that session to staff's limits from its next request. An account may hold any number of sessions at once.
   **Why:** NIST sets session limits by assurance level, at most 30 days overall at AAL1 and 24 hours overall with 1 hour idle at AAL2 (outside the repository: NIST SP 800-63B-4, sections 2.1.3 and 2.2.3), and ASVS gives no numbers of its own but asks for the reasoning where NIST is not followed (outside the repository: ASVS 5.0, 7.1.1). A student asks questions and keeps conversations, an AAL1 risk, and stays signed in across a week of lectures; staff change other people's courses and roles, and are held to AAL2's limits though they sign in with a password alone ([security.md](security.md), *Accepted risks*). The applications compared range from LibreChat's 7-day refresh under an absolute cap to Onyx's sliding 7 days with none and Open WebUI's 4 weeks (outside the repository: their source); a tier read per request is what lets one site hold both kinds of user.
   **The price:** staff sign in at least once a day, and again after an hour away; a student's session idle for a week ends.
3. **An account is opened by its owner, by registering or through an invitation, and its password is set by its owner alone, a forgotten one through a link; every door deactivates and deletes through the same rules.** The admin's add and password pages answer 403 (`apps/accounts/admin.py`), and every password set goes through one check (`apps/accounts/passwords.py`). Whoever may grant a role on a scope makes an invitation, a link that grants it once, which its holder accepts with an account they make on the spot or with the one they are signed in to (`apps/roles/invitations.py`); a superuser makes an account's reset link, with which its owner chooses a new password (`apps/accounts/links.py`). A link works once, an invitation for 72 hours and a reset link for 24; its maker hands it over, since the site sends no mail, its token travels in the address's fragment, and the server keeps only the token's digest. Deactivating ends the account's sessions; nobody takes away their own access; an account is deactivated before it is deleted, since deleting takes its conversations with it, and an owner deletes their own account with their password unless it holds a role or is the last active superuser (`apps/accounts/services.py`).
   **Why:** a password an administrator picks is one somebody else knows, which ASVS rules out at its highest level (outside the repository: ASVS 5.0, 6.4.6), and the admin's own forms skip the password checks registration applies; ASVS asks that an initial secret be random, short-lived and good for one use (6.4.1), which both links are. Dify's invitations last 72 hours and are deleted on activation, GitHub lists an expired invitation until it is cancelled, and NIST lets a recovery code sent by email live 24 hours (outside the repository: Dify's source, GitHub's documentation, NIST SP 800-63B-4, section 4.2.1.2). A browser sends no fragment to a server (outside the repository: RFC 3986, section 3.5), so no log of the site records a token. Onyx and RAGFlow also refuse to delete an account that is active (outside the repository: their source); a role is revoked on its scope's page before its holder leaves, and the last superuser is the only one who can manage accounts.
   **The price:** with no mail, a link reaches its holder only by hand, and a forgotten password waits for a superuser; a lost link is replaced by making another, since nobody can show it again.

## 2026-10-27 — A conversation and its messages are named by random UUIDs, and a thread is a chain of parents

1. **A conversation's id and a message's id are version 4 UUIDs the server makes, and the API writes them as strings.** `Conversation.id` and `Message.id` default to `uuid.uuid4` (`apps/qa/models.py`), and a conversation opens at `/chat/<id>` (`frontend/src/App.tsx`). An address whose id is not a UUID resolves to nothing, a 404; a `conversation_id` in a question's body that is not a UUID string, a number included, is a 400 on that field; a well-formed id naming nothing of the caller's is the 404 someone else's gets ([api.md](api.md)).
   **Why:** an integer key in an address tells any reader how many conversations the site holds. The chat applications this interface follows name a conversation by a random UUID: ChatGPT and Claude in their addresses (observed), Open WebUI and vercel/chatbot as its primary key, LibreChat as a public id beside its database key (outside the repository: their source). Version 4 rather than version 7, whose first 48 bits are the moment the id was made (outside the repository: RFC 9562, section 5.7), because when a conversation was started is what an address should not tell. Access does not rest on the id: an owner check answers every read, and a conversation that is not the caller's gets the answer of one that does not exist, as OWASP asks of a direct object reference (outside the repository: OWASP, *Insecure Direct Object Reference Prevention Cheat Sheet*). A malformed field in a body is the client's mistake, which Hugging Face chat-ui refuses with a 400 (outside the repository: its source), while an address names a resource, which either exists or does not.
   **The price:** the qa migration was rewritten and the local database rebuilt from a dump whose ids were converted ([development.md](development.md)). A key of 16 bytes where it was 8, inserted in random order.
2. **A message names its parent and a conversation its current leaf, and a thread is the path from the root to that leaf.** A question hangs from the leaf the conversation had when it was written, an answer from its question, and the answer becomes the leaf, all under the conversation's row lock (`record_exchange`, `apps/qa/conversations.py`). The thread a reader reopens and the history the next question carries are both that path (`thread`), read in one query and walked in memory; a chain that does not lead back to a root is a 500, never a shorter thread. A message is deleted only with its conversation or its owner: every message of a thread is the leaf or has it among its replies, and `Conversation.current_leaf` restricts the leaf's deletion to one that takes the conversation too.
   **Why:** with random ids, `(created_at, id)` orders the two rows of one exchange by chance when they share a timestamp, so the order has to be stored. A tree is how the chat applications that edit and regenerate store it: Open WebUI's `parentId` with `history.currentId`, LibreChat's `parentMessageId`, Dify's `parent_message_id` (outside the repository: their message models). `#217` adds the branches on it.
   **The price:** a thread is walked in Python, cheap at tens of rows and a recursive query at thousands; a broken chain fails the read rather than showing what it can; that a parent lies in the same conversation rests on its one writer, not on a database constraint.

## 2026-10-12 — A result list holds each passage once, as many as the reranker judges relevant, and an answer cites them by number

1. **Retrieval ranks equal scores by point id inside each branch and after the fusion, which runs on the client.** Each branch, dense or sparse, is a query of its own that asks for one point more than it keeps, doubles while that point ties with the last one kept, and ranks by score and then point id (`branch_search`, `rag/search.py`); the two branches merge by Reciprocal Rank Fusion with Qdrant's own formula, 1 / (rank + 2) with ranks counted from 0, and equal fused scores rank by point id too (`fuse_rrf`). The dense-only and sparse-only arms of `#25` read `branch_search`.
   **Why:** a Qdrant server searches its segments in parallel and returns equal scores in no fixed order, and one text under two addresses is two points with equal vectors, so after a restart a tie at a branch's cut moved a point across the top 5 ([experiment-log.md](experiment-log.md), 2026-09-29, section 4). Qdrant's fusion does not break ties either (outside the repository: Qdrant v1.19.2, `lib/segment/src/common/reciprocal_rank_fusion.rs`, "Does not break ties"; its hybrid-queries documentation, "zero-based rank positions"), and a search engine's own guide warns that results without a tiebreaker "could miss or duplicate hits" (outside the repository: Elasticsearch, *Paginate search results*). A fusion on the client is also where `#31` tunes the constant and the weights.
   **The price:** two requests per collection instead of one, more when a tie runs past the cut. The client sums in double precision where the server sums in single; the order of every fused score that is not a tie is the server's (`tests/test_search.py` compares the two on the embedded index).
2. **The copies of one passage collapse into one hit.** Two web hits are one passage when their texts are equal, or, from two pages or two versions of one page, when they share at least half their 3-word shingles (Jaccard ≥ 0.5, a PDF's hyphenated line breaks joined first) and every number of the shorter text is also in the longer, so that two exam calendars or two years' reports of one template stay apart. Two chunks of one page version are two paragraphs, and slides never collapse: one PDF in two editions is two identities ([data-model.md](data-model.md)). Pairs of hits that are one passage join their groups pair by pair, the most alike first, as single-linkage clustering does in Kruskal's order, so a chain of editions is one passage whatever their ranks; a join that would put two paragraphs of one page version into one group is refused, so a copy of one of them at another address, near both, goes with the one it is closer to. The group keeps its best score and place and shows its newest copy: the newest year when every copy's file name tells one, else the latest fetch, else the best rank. With the reranker the whole pool is reranked, collapsed, then cut, so a copy never takes another passage's slot; without it each collection's pool collapses before the round robin; the agent collapses again once the pages it fetched live have joined (`rag/passages.py`, `rag/search.py`, `rag/agent.py`).
   **Why:** 35% of the points of `unifi_web` share their text with a point at another URL ([experiment-log.md](experiment-log.md), 2026-10-12), and the question of `#187` was answered from four copies of one paragraph out of five sources. Shingle resemblance is how web search finds near-duplicate pages (outside the repository: Broder, "On the resemblance and containment of documents", 1997; Manku, Jain and Das Sarma, "Detecting near-duplicates for web crawling", WWW 2007); Google leaves out near-identical results by default (outside the repository: the `filter` parameter of the Programmable Search JSON API), and Open WebUI and Dify drop retrieved documents of equal content (outside the repository: Open WebUI, `retrieval/utils.py`; Dify, `_deduplicate_documents` in `retrieval_service.py`). The dense cosine cannot tell them apart: copies score 0.939 to 0.999, and two different paragraphs of one page reach 0.97 at the 99th percentile. The threshold and the number guard were chosen on `gold/near-duplicates.jsonl`, 276 pairs labelled before the rule was written, as the lowest Jaccard that joins the copies of `#187`'s passage, which share 25 of 50 shingles.
   **The price:** one of the 65 different pairs joins, the same procedure article in the Erasmus call and in the call for students from outside the EU, and 42 of the 211 same pairs stay apart (`uv run python -m rag.passages --measure gold/near-duplicates.jsonl`). A false join also joins what each text is a copy of: two paragraphs of one template whose older copies elsewhere are alike enough to join can show, on a page holding both, as one of the two twice. Showing the newest copy disfavours a question about one given year; the number guard keeps apart the editions whose dates differ, so such a question mostly keeps its copy. A citation points to a page that holds the cited text, since the survivor is one of the copies, and the campus gold set lists the verified aliases of its answer pages.
3. **How many sources an answer gets follows the reranker: a hit below P("yes") 0.5 is left out, the best one always stays, and five is a cap.** `RELEVANCE_CUTOFF` in `rag/search.py`, passed by the product (`apps/qa/engine.py`) and the CLIs; without a reranker nothing is cut, and `rag.gold` scores the first five unless `--min-score` is given.
   **Why:** the reranker's score is the probability that it answers "yes" to whether the document meets the query (outside the repository: the Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B model card), so 0.5 is where it says yes rather than no; Dify cuts a reranked list at a threshold whose default is 0.5, and Open WebUI filters on the reranker's score (outside the repository: Dify, `rerank_model.py` and `use-indexing-config.ts`; Open WebUI, `RAG_RELEVANCE_THRESHOLD`). The scores are bimodal: of the reranked top 5 of the 72 gold questions, 269 of 360 are 0.9 or more and 40 below 0.1. Fusion scores are ranks, not judgements, and are not cut.
   **The price:** a passage the reranker scores below 0.5 no longer reaches the answer unless it ranks first. Qwen publishes no threshold; the cut is tuned with k in `#31`.
4. **An answer cites an excerpt by its number, `[n]`, and names it no other way.** Each excerpt in the prompt is headed `[n]` beside its marker, and the model writes `[2]` after a claim and `[1][3]` for several, never a word for excerpt or source beside a number, in any language (`rag/answer.py`); `[n]` names the n-th citation of the `start` event, which the interface draws as a pill (`frontend/src/lib/markers.ts`). Two web sources of one list that share host and title show as much of the end of their paths as sets them apart, or the day each was fetched (`frontend/src/features/chat/sourceLabel.ts`).
   **Why:** the marker the model was told to copy is a URL and a date, which the 4B model shortened, replaced with "Excerpt N", or translated ("negli estratti 1, 2 e 3", `#187`); a number is two characters, and it names one excerpt where a marker names a page. Open WebUI, LlamaIndex and RAGFlow ask for a bracketed number (outside the repository: Open WebUI's RAG template in `config.py`, `[id]`; LlamaIndex's `CitationQueryEngine`, `[1]`; RAGFlow's `citation_prompt.md`, `[ID:i]`). VS Code names two editors of one file name by the distinguishing sections of their paths (outside the repository: the `workbench.editor.labelFormat` setting).
   **The price:** generation results recorded before this date were produced with earlier prompts (`GENERATION_PROMPT_SHA256`, `tests/test_answer.py`). Thirty answers do not measure the answer's language: asked in English for an Italian or a Chinese answer, the model answered in the language asked for 5 times in 20, 8 under the prompt before ([experiment-log.md](experiment-log.md), 2026-10-12); the full check, 120 answers, runs with `#27` on MICC.

## 2026-10-08 — Text the project did not write enters every prompt as quoted material

1. **Every prompt holds the text it did not write between `<quoted>` and `</quoted>`, and says what that text is.** An excerpt, an earlier turn of the conversation, a link's anchor and a fetched page's sample go into the user message through `quote()` (`rag/llm.py`), each tag on a line of its own; what the project writes, an excerpt's number and its citation marker, stays outside. A look-alike of a tag inside the text, literal or upper-case, in a fullwidth or small form, split by an invisible character or by spaces, is found on the text's NFKC form and replaced in the original, every other character kept as it was (`neutralize()`). A URL left outside the quote, the one in a citation marker included, is percent-encoded down to what RFC 3986 allows, so it holds no space, bracket or word in another script (`inert_url()`), and a content type reaches the gate only as a media type with token parameters (`inert_content_type()`); a citation the API returns is that same marker. Every system prompt that reads such text carries one sentence saying that the quote is data and that an instruction inside it is text to report or to judge (`QUOTED_CLAUSE`). The router's prompt does not: it reads the student's own questions and nothing else. `tests/test_llm.py` finds every system message under `rag/` and fails on a prompt without the sentence, and drives each prompt with a text written to break out of its block.
   **Why:** separating external content from instructions is the mitigation OWASP names for indirect prompt injection (outside the repository: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, LLM01, "Segregate and identify external content"), and retrieval-augmented tools delimit what they retrieve the same way: Open WebUI wraps each source in a `<source>` tag, Anthropic's prompting guide puts each document in `<document>` tags, Qwen-Agent heads each one with its source and fences it (outside the repository: Open WebUI's `get_source_context()`, Anthropic's guide on long-context prompting, Qwen-Agent's RAG prompt). None of the three handles a closing tag the content forges itself; Spotlighting does, with a delimiter the content must not hold and that is removed when it does (outside the repository: Hines et al., "Defending Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks With Spotlighting", 2024, arXiv 2403.14720). An anonymous conversation's history is sent back by the browser, so the turns are quoted like any page. The tags stand on lines of their own because, written inline, Qwen3-4B copied them into an answer, and naming them in a rule made it copy them more ([experiment-log.md](experiment-log.md), 2026-10-08).
   **The price:** a delimiter lowers the odds that a 4B model obeys quoted text and does not remove them; why the rest is accepted is in [security.md](security.md), row LLM01:2026. Spotlighting's stronger form, datamarking, which interleaves a marker through every word of the quoted text, is left out: it is unmeasured on Qwen3-4B and may cost answer quality, so it is a comparison for M3 rather than a default. The deepening loop's pick declared two shortlists out of three unsuitable where the prompt before chose a link, a change no labelled set can judge.

## 2026-10-07 — The interface takes the university's blue on greys tinted toward it

1. **The palette carries one hue, the university's blue, on greys of the same hue.** `--accent` is `#004C7F`, the university website's own `--main-blue` (outside the repository: the stylesheet of www.unifi.it, read on 7 October 2026), its screen value of the institutional Pantone 301 that the visual identity manual names, C94 M58 Y18 K0 in print; the dark theme takes a lighter step of the same hue. The greys lean toward that hue at a chroma low enough to read as grey, as the website's `#F4F5F6` and `#212529` do, and `--accent-soft`, a fill tinted with the blue, marks where a reader is. The blue goes where something can be done or names the project: the primary button, the focus ring, links, the open conversation, the mark. An answer's text stays in ink; a citation pill says it can be clicked by its fill, `--mark` at rest and ink while its card is open, never by the blue, which inside an answer would read as an ordinary link (`frontend/src/features/chat/AnswerMarkdown.tsx`); `--warn*` stays for a failure and for confirming a destruction. The values are in `frontend/src/index.css`, and `frontend/scripts/check-contrast.mjs` holds them to the one hue.
   **Why:** after a thesis meeting the supervisor found the interface too bare and allowed the university's colour ([architecture.md](architecture.md), "The supervisor's constraints"); the visual identity manual's prohibition concerns the logo, not the colour (`#128`). The website gives the colour and nothing else: its blue band, uppercase navigation and square frames would make a lab tool read as an official page, which the university's trademark guidelines do not allow (outside the repository: *Linee guida per l'uso del marchio*, 1.4). Layout and richness follow the AI products the interface measures itself against, which spend their accent the same way: on what can be acted on, never on the reading.
   **The price:** blue means "act here" or "this is the project", so a blue word inside an answer would read as a link; the reading stays in ink for that reason, and the citation link stays on fill.

## 2026-10-05 — The staff pages live in the chat shell, where a course is offered is open to all staff, and the API serves only what a page reads

1. **The staff pages are a "Gestione" group in the chat's sidebar, not an area with a top bar of its own.** One layout route, `frontend/src/routes/ShellLayout.tsx`, holds the sidebar for the chat and for every `/staff/` page, so moving between them neither reloads the conversation list nor mounts a second sidebar. The group's items follow from the superuser flag, the programmes the caller may view and whether the account teaches an edition (`frontend/src/features/staff/landing.ts`, `frontend/src/auth/staff.ts`), while what a page offers to do follows from each row's `permissions`: the superuser, and secretariat staff of two programmes or more, get the list of programmes; secretariat staff of exactly one go straight to their programme; a teacher also gets their own courses. `/staff` lands on the first item.
   **Why:** the pages follow the prototype shown to the supervisor (outside the repository), whose secretariat opens on its programme. A list of one is a page with nothing to choose, so the rule carries over to staff of several programmes, rather than an item per programme, which would grow the sidebar with the scope and label each item with a programme's name. Learning platforms put the staff area in the navigation everyone uses, shown to whoever holds it: Moodle 4.0's primary navigation carries "Site administration" beside "Home", "Dashboard" and "My courses", and Canvas's global navigation an "Admin" tray listing the accounts an administrator manages (outside the repository: Moodle 4.0 release notes; Canvas guide "How do I use the Global Navigation Menu as an admin?"). A route with no path that renders its children in an `<Outlet>` is React Router's layout route (outside the repository: React Router documentation, "Routing").
   **The price:** the chat page tells the layout, through its context, when the conversations changed and which one is being answered (`frontend/src/routes/shell.ts`). Leaving a chat while it answers stops the answer: the stream is the chat page's own request, which unmounting aborts (`frontend/src/features/chat/useAsk.ts`), and any route away from the chat unmounts it, shared layout or not. The answer is kept as far as it got and marked incomplete (`Message`, `apps/qa/models.py`).
2. **Which programmes list a course is open to all staff; a programme's own plan, page and staff stay behind its scope.** `GET /api/catalog/programmes/<code>/courses` is a programme's plan, a course with no edition included. A course, wherever the API returns it — on its own, in a plan, inside an edition — carries its entries in every programme that lists it, so a teacher reads, through their edition, the names of the other programmes offering the course. A programme outside the caller's scope is a 404, its page, its plan and its secretariat alike. A course is read by whoever may view a programme that lists it (`courses_for`, `apps/roles/scopes.py`); a teacher reads no course page, and reaches their editions through their own courses.
   **Why:** the study plans are the public Cineca course catalogue (outside the repository: `unifi.coursecatalogue.cineca.it`), so hiding which programmes list a course protects nothing a browser would not show; the 404 protects what the catalogue does not publish, who holds a role where. The registry has no course scope (2026-09-29, *Staff permissions are a registry in code, answered by one backend; the admin is the superuser's*, point 1): a course is reached through a programme, and a teacher reaches their courses through the editions they cover.
   **The price:** every course the API returns carries its entries, read at a fixed number of queries however many there are (`tests/test_catalog_api.py`).
3. **The API serves only the reads a page makes.** The edition list has one filter, `?course=`, and a member collection (`…/teachers`, `…/secretariat`) answers `POST` alone: staff are read on their scope's own row, `teachers` on an edition and `secretariat` on a programme.
   **Why:** every endpoint resolves a scope and is tested for every caller, so one that no page calls is a surface nobody watches, and a second read of the same rows can drift from the first.
   **The price:** a reader the SPA does not have, such as a script, adds its endpoint and its tests when it appears.

## 2026-10-05 — The site is a lab site, so the interface may go as far as showcase sites, on three floors

1. **The interface may go as far as the best showcase sites: 3D, scroll-driven motion, a guided first visit.** The site is a small, non-commercial site of the MICC lab, not the university's official website, and the interface is graded (*A person confirms what automatic growth proposes; the interface is graded*, 2026-09-14, point 2). What to borrow is triaged per reference in `#135` (visuals, motion, 3D), `#136` (first visit) and `#137` (chat interaction); `#128` picks the style and the university colour.
   **Why:** a lab's demonstration site is not treated as a university service, so the interface is held to the floors of point 2 rather than to the reference of the M4 milestone alone, and the thesis gains from an interface that shows craft. The showcase sites pay for it: unseen.co loads about 7 MB first and ignores reduced motion (outside the repository: measured in YoZoLabs/airjump-website issue 42).
   **The price:** a heavier, more complex frontend; an effect that cannot pass the floors is dropped, however good it looks.
2. **Three floors hold every effect, checked by a browser job that runs in CI only (`#134`).** Nothing moves when the system asks for reduced motion; axe passes on every route in both themes; the chat's first paint stays within a byte budget, and heavy assets such as three.js load after it and are excluded by name.
   **Why:** students open the site to ask a question, often on a phone, so the question must not wait for a scene, and motion must yield to the system setting. Browser tests stay out of the local loop, which has to stay fast.
   **The price:** a Playwright job, and API fixtures kept in step with the TypeScript mirrors of the contract.

## 2026-09-29 — Staff permissions are a registry in code, answered by one backend; the admin is the superuser's

1. **Permissions are a registry in code, and one authentication backend answers them.** `apps/roles/registry.py` names the permissions, each on one kind of scope — a programme or an edition — and which role holds which. `RoleBackend` (`apps/roles/backends.py`) comes after Django's `ModelBackend` and overrides `get_user_permissions` alone, so `user.has_perm(permission, edition)`, its async twin and `get_all_permissions` give one answer. Which role covers which scope is written once, in `apps/roles/scopes.py`: a teacher covers every edition of a course they are assigned to an edition of, the years before and after their own alike, secretariat staff every edition of every course their programme offers, and the lists and each item's `permissions` read that same resolution. A teacher row names one edition, so who taught which year stays recorded. An inactive account, an unknown permission, a permission of the other kind of scope and a missing object are refused; an active superuser is answered yes by Django before any backend is asked.
   **Why:** Sentry fixes its organisation roles and their scopes in code, `SENTRY_ROLES`, and openedx-authz defines its roles in code as `RoleData` (outside the repository: their source). Django's hook for per-object permissions is a backend whose permission methods take the object, the way django-guardian and django-rules plug in (outside the repository: Django 5.2 documentation, "Customizing authentication"). The ready-made options were turned down: DRF's `DjangoObjectPermissions` needs Django's model permissions, which have no scope; django-rules has no release declaring Django 5.2 support; django-guardian stores a row per object and permission, a second copy of `RoleAssignment`. A teacher reaches the whole course because the supervisor asked that a course's new teacher reach what its earlier editions hold ([architecture.md](architecture.md), section "The supervisor's constraints"), and a demonstration gains nothing from a read-only tier for past years. The platforms checked do not do this by default: Moodle, Canvas, Open edX, Google Classroom and D2L Brightspace open an offering's content to the staff of that offering, and another offering only through an explicit grant, such as a Moodle category role, an Open edX organisation role or a D2L parent template (outside the repository: their documentation and source, read 2026-10-13). The widening here is deliberate.
   **The price:** a teacher of one year changes every other year of the course, the edition of whoever teaches it next included, until their row is revoked. Two authentication backends, so a `login()` that did not go through `authenticate()` names the one it uses (`RegisterView`, `apps/accounts/views.py`), and their order matters: the test client's `force_login()` records the first backend that has a `get_user()`, and only `ModelBackend`'s loads anyone.
2. **A student's programme is not a role row.** Where the programme is stored, a field on `User` or a profile of its own, is `#36`'s, the issue that first reads it.
   **Why:** a role grants something, and the programme protects nothing: it narrows a search and is self-declared. Open edX keeps a learner's enrollment, `CourseEnrollment`, apart from course staff roles, `CourseAccessRole` (outside the repository: Open edX source).
   **The price:** a second place that scopes a user, beside `RoleAssignment`.
3. **The admin admits superusers only and repairs data, and `is_staff` never follows from a role.** `SuperuserAdminSite` (`config/admin.py`) is the default admin site through `config/apps.py`; a staff account that is not a superuser is sent back to the admin's login page, which says it is not authorized. A superuser works in the SPA like other staff: accounts are listed, deactivated, reactivated, signed out everywhere, handed a reset link and deleted at `/staff/users` (`apps/accounts/manage_views.py`), and roles granted or offered by invitation on their scope's page (point 4). The admin is where a superuser corrects a row no page reaches, role rows by username among them (`apps/roles/admin.py`); staff work in the SPA, within their scope.
   **Why:** the admin edits every row of every table, so a scope cannot be drawn in it, and Django's documentation limits its recommended use to an organisation's internal management tool (outside the repository: Django 5.2 documentation, "The Django admin site", and its section on overriding the default admin site). Saleor, Sentry and Zulip do not install Django's admin at all, and their staff work in the application itself; Open edX course teams work in Studio; GitLab's administrators manage users from the application's own Admin area, its API of the same shape (outside the repository: their source, and GitLab's "Users API" documentation).
   **The price:** staff do through the site only what their pages offer (2026-10-05, *The staff pages live in the chat shell*); entering the catalogue is the superuser's job, in the admin.
4. **An assign table decides who grants what.** `GRANT_PERMISSION` (`apps/roles/registry.py`) names the permission that grants each role on its scope: secretariat staff assign teachers in their programme's editions, and no role holds the permission that assigns secretariat staff, so only the superuser does. Staff are granted and revoked under their scope (`apps/catalog/staff_views.py`) and read on their scope's own row (2026-10-05, *The staff pages live in the chat shell*, point 3), the scope resolved before the permission and the permission before the request body; each grant and revocation made there writes one line to the `apps.roles.grants` log once it commits, while the superuser's changes in the admin stay in the admin's own history, and the demo seed, `manage.py populate_demo` (`apps/roles/management/commands/populate_demo.py`), which touches `demo-` accounts only, prints each role row it adds or removes.
   **Why:** Moodle decides who may assign which role with `role_allow_assign` and fires a `role_assigned` event on each assignment, and Open edX checks the caller's authority on the course before changing its staff, in `_check_caller_authority` (outside the repository: their source). The subset rule of Saleor, Sentry and Canvas — grant only what you hold — would stop secretariat staff from assigning teachers the day teachers held a permission the secretariat does not. GitLab and Canvas nest members under their scope, `/projects/:id/members` and `/courses/:id/enrollments` (outside the repository: their API documentation).
   **The price:** secretariat staff can make themselves a teacher of an edition in their programme, which grants nothing they lack, since they hold every teacher permission on their programme's editions, and the teacher row outlives their secretariat row, so revoking a person is done in the admin by username. An unknown username is a 400, which staff holding the assign permission can use to test whether an account exists, and the catalogue endpoints carry no throttle of their own.

## 2026-09-29 — A slides citation opens its whole PDF, and a conversation is deleted outright

1. **The PDF a slides citation came from is served whole and inline to any logged-in account, keyed by its sha256, and every miss is a 404.** `GET /api/sources/<sha256>` finds the file through the parse sidecars and serves it only from inside the corpus directory, never from a path the client sends (`apps/qa/sources.py`); a slide's pill and its row in the sources panel link it at `#page=N`, so the browser's own viewer opens the cited page.
   **Why:** Open WebUI and Onyx open a cited file the same way — by id, behind authentication, in the browser's viewer (outside the repository: Open WebUI and Onyx source). An in-page viewer would ship pdf.js, whose worker alone is 374 kB gzipped, more than the whole first-load bundle. A 404 for a key the endpoint will not serve does not confirm the content exists; Onyx answers a file the caller may not read with a 404 for the same reason.
   **The price:** registration is open, so anyone who registers can download whole decks, more than the excerpts `POST /api/ask` shows. `#36` limits this endpoint to the decks of courses in the caller's programme, past editions included, which is consistency with retrieval and not protection: the programme is self-declared ([data-model.md](data-model.md), scope invariant 3). What narrows the exposure is the registration policy and the roles of `#93`. The sidecars record absolute paths, so the corpus is served only on the machine that parsed it.
2. **A student deletes a conversation outright: `DELETE /api/conversations/<id>` removes the row, and its messages go with it (`CASCADE`).** The delete and the writing of a question's two messages lock the same conversation row, so whichever comes second sees what the first committed; a question that waited in the queue while its conversation was deleted is refused with 400 before anything is written, and the answer slot is freed at once (`apps/qa/conversation_views.py`, `apps/qa/conversations.py`). There is no rename, archive or soft delete.
   **Why:** Open WebUI, LibreChat, vercel/chatbot and LobeChat delete a conversation outright (outside the repository: their source). M3 reads the gold set and `data/webcorpus/decisions.jsonl`, not the `Message` table, so no evaluation depends on keeping deleted rows.
   **The price:** the incomplete answers of a deleted conversation, which the `Message` docstring calls raw material for M3's error taxonomy, go with it; every question's write takes one more row-lock query.

## 2026-09-28 — Slides points carry their edition, and a re-index replaces a source file within it

1. **`rag.parse` requires `--course` and `--academic-year` instead of inferring the course from the target directory name.** Both flags are `required=True`; `main()` checks the pair against `rag.chunk.edition_of` before touching a file and refuses through `parser.error` on a malformed one.
   **Why:** a directory name (`PPM`) is not a course code, and re-parsing by directory name would write points that match neither the delete key nor the evaluation's edition.
   **The price:** every parse spells out the edition.
2. **A re-index replaces a slides file by its source, (`source_file`, `course`, `academic_year`), and writes before it deletes.** `index_chunks` upserts a file's new points, then `replace_slides_sources` deletes every other point under that key (`rag/index.py`); the sha in `chunk_id` only says whether the content changed. Web chunks follow the same order under their (`url`, `ingest_source`) pair.
   **Why:** deleting by sha, a file whose content changed looks for its new sha and never finds the old points, so both versions stay retrievable. LangChain's indexing API names a document by `source_id_key`, and its `incremental` cleanup removes the stale versions of the sources it saw during the run (outside the repository: LangChain source, `langchain_core/indexing/api.py`). An unmerged Dify pull request, #37068, reports vectors left behind when deletion recomputed ids instead of filtering on a stable field (outside the repository). Writing first means a file is never missing from the index; a crash between the two steps leaves extra points that the next run removes.
   **The price:** one edition of one PDF has one parse variant in a collection.
3. **Indexing a directory syncs the editions it holds.** After a directory run in which no file failed, `sync_editions` deletes, for each edition the run saw, the points whose `source_file` is none of the directory's files (`rag/index.py`); two files carrying one source file of one edition count as a failed file, and a single file never deletes another. This is LangChain's `full` cleanup limited to the editions seen in the run. LangChain's cleanup is an explicit `cleanup` argument (outside the repository: LangChain source, `langchain_core/indexing/api.py`); here it is implicit, because deleted and renamed files must not stay retrievable.
   **Why:** a deck removed or renamed in the corpus directory would otherwise keep answering.
   **The price:** a directory must hold an edition's whole set of files; to update some files only, index them one by one. The run logs the files it deletes before deleting them.

## 2026-09-28 — An AD code belongs to one course, and codes and years are written in ASCII

1. **An AD code belongs to one course.** `CurriculumEntry.clean()` refuses an `ad_code` that another course's entry holds, or that is another course's `Course.code`; `Course.clean()` refuses a `code` an entry of another course holds as its `ad_code`. A course's own code, listed as its own entry's `ad_code` — PPM's TECNICO APPLICATIVO row, `ad_code="B028451"` — is not a conflict.
   **Why:** Cineca's catalogue opens one teaching per AD code, so one code cannot answer to two courses. Canvas validates its own analogue, `sis_source_id`, at the application layer — a Rails `uniqueness` validation plus a pre-save check, not a database exclusion constraint (outside the repository: Canvas LMS source). Two database-level alternatives were turned down: a dedicated `AdCode` table, since nothing reads an AD code except through its owning `CurriculumEntry` or `Course`; and a PostgreSQL exclusion constraint, which needs the `btree_gist` extension to guard rows that people enter through the admin, where `full_clean()` runs in any case.
   **The price:** `loaddata` and the ORM's `.create()` skip `clean()`, so a fixture or a script that bypasses `full_clean()` can write a conflicting AD code; only a form or a script that calls it is protected.
2. **`Course.code`, `CurriculumEntry.ad_code` and `CourseEdition.academic_year` are written in ASCII, and `year_of_study` is bounded, each by a database constraint.** The constraints are `catalog_course_code_format`, `catalog_entry_ad_code_format`, `catalog_edition_academic_year_format` and `catalog_entry_year_of_study_range` (`apps/catalog/models.py`); their character classes are the ASCII `[A-Z0-9]` and `[0-9]`, never a `\d` class, whose reach past ASCII depends on collation. The patterns and the range are owned by [data-model.md](data-model.md), scope invariant 1.
   **Why:** PostgreSQL's documentation on its character classes states that whether a non-ASCII character belongs to a class such as `\d` depends on collation, and only the `C` locale excludes every non-ASCII character from it; `[0-9]` matches the ten ASCII digits regardless of collation (outside the repository: PostgreSQL documentation, pattern matching). A trailing space or a stray `:` in a code would break `CODE:YEAR`, the string both `rag/` and the command line parse.
   **The price:** an official code printed with a character outside `[A-Z0-9]` — a hyphen, an accent — needs the constraint widened before it can be entered.

## 2026-09-25 — The data model is decided on paper before roles are built on it

1. **A course has two levels and a yearly edition.** A `DegreeProgramme` (such as `B047`) lists `Course` rows (such as `B003725`) through `CurriculumEntry`, which carries `year_of_study`, the `ad_code` and the curriculum, so a course shared between programmes (*mutuazione*) is one more entry pointing at the same course, never a copy of its material. Material, syllabus, reading list and reference pages hang on a `CourseEdition` (`course`, `academic_year` such as `2025-2026`); past editions are kept and can be chosen. Which edition is current is derived, never stored: the newest one whose teaching has started, by its `teaching_starts` or, with none entered, 1 September of its first year, so a course has at most one by construction; a search defaults to it. The slides payload field `course` is the UniFi course code, and `academic_year` sits beside it.
   **Why:** this is how UniFi's own Moodle lists a course — `B003725 (B047)`, the programme as its category, shared courses marked *mutuati* — and Cineca Esse3, Moodle categories and Canvas accounts nest the same way; UniFi's Moodle opens one container per academic year, as Open edX does with course runs and Canvas with terms (outside the repository). A date and not a switch: Open edX takes a course's newest run that has started as the one it advertises, Moodle lists a course as in progress or past by its start and end dates, and Canvas by its term's (outside the repository: their source and documentation). A switch has to be remembered every year: the one the demo catalogue made left 2024-2025 current in October 2026. A new edition is worth searching once its material is in, and the material comes in as its teaching starts.
   **The price:** an edition whose start nobody entered counts from 1 September, which for a second-semester course is about five months early, and an edition whose material is late is current all the same. The evaluation pins PPM's code and year instead of following the current edition, so a new academic year cannot move an M3 number.
   **Status:** 🔶 the four tables and the admin are `apps/catalog/models.py`, `apps/catalog/admin.py`, and the current edition is `with_current()` in `apps/catalog/editions.py`, which the catalogue API reads (`apps/catalog/views.py`); the slides payload carries the code and year (`rag/chunk.py`), and so do the stored points. Missing: a search defaulting to the current edition, `#36` (M5).
2. **Roles are rows with a scope; permissions are code.** `RoleAssignment` (`user`, `role`, nullable `programme`, nullable `edition`) holds the staff scopes: a teacher is assigned to a `CourseEdition` and reassigned every year, the secretariat to a `DegreeProgramme`. What each role may do is a permission registry in code, and views check a permission, never a role name — the one-gate-per-action rule of `#92`. The site administrator is Django's `is_superuser`, not a role row. A student's programme is not a role row, and where it is stored is `#36`'s (2026-09-29, *Staff permissions are a registry in code, answered by one backend*, point 2). That programme is **a scope boundary, self-declared until `#44`**: it bounds the courses a student's search may cover, and it is not a security boundary, because the student states it.
   **Why:** one table with nullable scope columns is Open edX's `CourseAccessRole`; a role-to-permission registry in code is Sentry's; teachers per term course and staff per category are Canvas enrollments and Moodle role assignments at category level (outside the repository). A teacher row names an edition and not the course because of point 1: who teaches is decided year by year, as the edition's own fact. What the row covers is every edition of the course (2026-09-29, *Staff permissions are a registry in code, answered by one backend; the admin is the superuser's*, point 1).
   **The price:** teacher assignments are repeated every year (the secretariat copies the previous year's rows), and until `#44` brings the university identity nothing stops a student from declaring another programme.
   **Status:** 🔶 `RoleAssignment`, the registry and the backend that answers it are `apps/roles/`; copying the previous year's teacher rows is `#93` (M5).
3. **Anonymous students ask campus questions only, and nothing of theirs is stored.** Their multi-turn history lives in the browser and travels with each request; the server treats it as untrusted input and keeps no row, so `Conversation.owner` stays non-nullable and no migration follows. Course questions and stored conversations stay behind login.
   **Why:** chat products that admit anonymous users keep the thread on the server under a cookie, HuggingChat under a session cookie, Vercel's chatbot template under a guest user row, Perplexity under a visitor id (outside the repository), because that buys cross-device history, analytics and a path to sign-up. None of those is what the anonymous tier is for here, and keeping nothing is the data minimisation the GDPR asks for (article 5(1)(c)). Keeping nothing is how the stateless APIs work: the OpenAI Chat Completions API takes the whole `messages` list on every call, and its Responses API keeps nothing under `store: false` (outside the repository). The campus corpus is public pages, so an anonymous reader learns nothing a browser would not show.

A course is taught to everyone enrolled in it, so its material is shared by construction.
   **The price:** a student cannot add their own notes (point 7). A student reading the syllabus itself reads it in the teacher's language: only the answers come in the student's.

   **Why:** an ISO or NIST mapping holds where a real audit report stands behind it; this repository has none, so the mapping would be invented. The two OWASP lists assume no audit, and a self-review is their intended use.

## 2026-08-27 — An error is a code the interface words; the server's message stays English

1. **Each language layer has an owner**: the interface belongs to the three frontend catalogues (it, en, zh-hans), the answer language to the prompt in `rag/answer.py`, and the server's messages to whoever reads its log, in English and with no gettext catalogue, since a catalogue for the log would have no reader.
2. **An `/api/` error is a code from one closed table, and the page words it.** `config/errors.py` gives each code its HTTP status and an English message for the log; the body carries the code, the values its sentence fills in and, for each rejected field, a reason from a second table ([api.md](api.md), section "Errors"). No sentence of this project's, DRF's or Django's reaches a page: the page reads the code's sentence from the interface's catalogue, so an error reads in the interface's language and a language switch rewords one on screen. An error the reader cannot act on adds its code and the request's id, which names the server's log lines for that request.
   **Why:** a page can translate a code it knows, never a sentence it is sent, and DRF's own validation messages follow `Accept-Language` while this project's are English, so a refusal worded by the server could be in neither the interface's language nor one language at all (#186). Google's API design guide names errors by a closed set of codes, each with the details a client acts on (outside the repository: AIP-193). RFC 9457's problem details were turned down: none of the open-source RAG applications compared uses them, and their `type` URI beside a code is a second identifier for one error; so was drf-standardized-errors, which covers neither a closed set of codes, the answer stream's `error` event nor a request id (outside the repository: their documentation).
   **The price:** a new error is a row in the table, a sentence in each of the three catalogues and, for a code of DRF's or Django's, a mapping, or the tests fail; a rejection the second table has no reason for reaches the reader as `INVALID`, saying less than the validator knew; and a page loaded before a deploy reads a code it does not know as `INTERNAL`.

## 2026-08-24 — Qdrant runs as a service, and every M3 baseline is measured on it

Every client opens the server at `QDRANT_URL` (`config/env.py`), and an embedded index only when given its directory (`--qdrant DIR`), the way Dify reads `QDRANT_URL` (outside the repository: Dify's Qdrant vector store); an empty value is an error, never a fallback. The move was gated on retrieval rather than on the automatic-growth remeasurement, which would have held back the Celery worker (`#34`) and with it the website: no point lost, approximate search as good as exact search (recall@20 1.000), and the same HIT or MISS on every gold question on the product's path, so the backend is not among the confounders of the automatic-growth score ([experiment-log.md](experiment-log.md), entry of 2026-09-29).

## 2026-08-04 — Sparse retrieval is fastembed BM25 with an IDF modifier; SPLADE is not pursued

1. **The sparse vector is fastembed's `Qdrant/bm25`: term frequency on the document side, with IDF applied by the collection (`modifier=IDF`).** Stemming is fastembed's default English scheme. The chunk keeps `text`, the sparse input, apart from `embed_text`, the dense input with the heading chain, so the two inputs do not contaminate each other. SPLADE and a self-built IDF are not pursued.
   **Why:** BM25 adds no second neural model to the ingest and query paths, and an IDF that Qdrant computes follows the collection as points are added, live ones included, with no re-encoding.
   **The price:** Italian and Chinese chunks are tokenised and stemmed as English, so the sparse side is weakest for them; the BM25 · dense · hybrid comparison of `#25` (M3) measures what that costs.
   **Status:** ✅ `rag/index.py`.
